# X copywriting

Write as a person talking to readers, not an article being filed for an archive. Clarity comes from explaining the actual proposal, not adding slogans or tables of prices.

## When to use

Draft, shorten, translate or edit an X/Twitter post, especially personal proposals, business ideas, crypto commentary or a draft rejected as AI slop. This skill writes text; publishing to X is a separate action requiring explicit authorization.

## Default delivery and voice

- Deliver one finished post directly in the message. No code block, preface, Russian explanation, alternative drafts or editing report unless requested.
- For this author, default to English, mostly lowercase, short sentences and numbered points when presenting several ideas. Preserve ticker spelling, e.g. $CLAUS. The latest request and any supplied voice sample override defaults.
- Premium+ permits a longer single post; it is not a reason to fill the space or create a thread. A compact proposal around 1,200–1,600 characters is a useful calibration, not a mandatory length for every topic.
- Speak to the audience. A natural opener may be "got a few ideas for [project]. bit of a brain dump, but curious what you think." Adapt it; do not reuse it as a compulsory template.
- First person should sound spontaneous. Do not start every point with "i'd", "i think" or "i would". Usually begin with the action itself.
- A relevant closing question can invite a real response, e.g. "which of these would you actually use?" Avoid generic engagement bait.

## Drafting workflow

1. **Lock the meaning before polishing.** From the latest brief, identify each distinct proposal, its audience, deliverable and reason someone would pay or care. Keep the user's order and distinctions unless asked to restructure. Later corrections win over all earlier drafts. Do not replace a user's service with a different one that sounds easier to price.
2. **Open each point with one short action sentence.** For example, "sell custom research on other projects' contracts and hooks." Avoid empty categories like "paid services".
3. **Explain what happens and why it might work.** Name the work and the result the customer gets. Add the practical reason: saves repeated checks, replaces operational work, or helps evaluate a contract. A heading plus numbers is not an explanation.
4. **Use numbers sparingly.** Usually one or two useful illustrative figures; at most two or three per point when requested. This is a ceiling per point, not a quota per sentence. Do not manufacture a price to make a point look concrete. Mark proposals with "say", "around", "could" or one short shared note. Do not present them as existing tariffs or market facts.
5. **Move shared rules out of the points.** One paragraph for buybacks, allocation or another common mechanism. Do not repeat the same cash-flow explanation four times or invent an allocation percentage the user has not chosen.
6. **Cut the second half of the draft.** Remove delivery procedures, reviewer budgets, gas thresholds, refunds, locks, expense waterfalls and multiple pricing tiers unless one is essential to the argument. If asked to halve length, measure source and draft with a tool. Keep all distinct ideas, audiences, material caveats and the common rule; cut repeated explanation first.
7. **Read aloud and check substance.** Vary sentence length. Replace jargon with actions. Delete corporate transitions, exaggerated claims, decorative headings, repeated negative contrasts and slogan endings. Apply humanizer privately; return only the final post, not its draft/audit/final output template.

## Meaning and factual checks

- Separate proposal from current capability. Never turn "could offer" into a claim that a project already sells the service.
- Approximate pricing is a testable suggestion, not evidence of demand. If actual costs or current API capabilities matter, verify them with an appropriate source. If an example amount is unnecessary, omit it rather than research an unrelated tariff.
- Distinguish revenue, margin and the amount available for buybacks. Avoid suggesting all gross revenue can buy tokens while ignoring operating costs. Preserve the agreed rule; do not quietly insert a 50% split, holder rights or guaranteed price impact.
- Keep another platform's fees separate from the proposed service revenue. Do not imply access to funds or control of contracts that has not been established.
- Review, research and monitoring are not automatically security audits or safety guarantees. Avoid selling an "approval" when the customer is paying for analysis.
- Paid data access alone is not a product. Explain the additional synthesis, structural analysis or recommendation. Do not claim every metric is available from one API or suggest unlicensed redistribution.
- Token/DAO management and revenue-model curation are different jobs. Recurring reports are distinct from a one-off investigation. Keep these distinctions when present in the brief.

## Failure patterns and repairs

| Failure | Repair |
| --- | --- |
| "paid services" followed by a fee | Name the action, deliverable and customer. |
| Every paragraph starts "i'd" | Start with the proposed action; use first person selectively. |
| Many precise tariffs make the idea seem rigorous | Keep only useful examples and label them as proposals. |
| A launch service becomes contract approval | Restore the user's actual launch/management proposal. |
| Subscription just repeats a custom report | Explain ongoing monitoring versus a one-off investigation. |
| All fee revenue goes to buybacks | Account for costs and keep the common allocation separate. |
| Premium+ turns a post into a manual | Use the room for clarity, not procedure. |
| Removing numbers also removes meaning | Preserve the work, customer benefit and business mechanism. |
| "brain dump" is repeated in every future opener | Match the conversation; the example is calibration, not a slogan. |

## Final check

- Same distinct proposals as the latest brief?
- Each point begins with a short proposal and explains both what and why?
- Numbers necessary, sparse and clearly hypothetical where appropriate?
- Common rule appears once, without invented percentages?
- Sounds like a person addressing readers rather than a consultant's article?
- Requested length checked with a tool when measurable?
- Single paste-ready post, no commentary or publication without permission?
